Craft over passion: why I keep changing lanes

"Follow your passion" is the most repeated career advice I know, and the one I trust the least.

I trust it least because I have watched it fail people. The friend who quit a job to chase a passion that evaporated the moment it became work. The graduate paralyzed because they were waiting to feel certain before they started. The advice assumes you arrive with a pre-installed passion, and that the job is to find the career that matches it. That is not how it has worked for me, and I do not think it is how it works for most people.

What I follow instead is the craft. I pick something hard and valuable, I get genuinely good at it, and I let the interest grow out of the competence. The passion is real. It just shows up second.

The order is backwards

Passion is the output of mastery, not the input. When you are bad at something, it is frustrating, not fulfilling. The fulfillment arrives later, once you are skilled enough to do work that matters and to be trusted with harder problems. People look at someone who loves their work and conclude they found the right thing. More often, they got good at a thing, and the love was the reward for the reps.

This reframes the whole question. The goal stops being "find what I love" and becomes "get so good at something useful that I earn the right to do interesting work." That is a craftsman's question, and it has an answer you can actually act on tomorrow morning.

Career capital

Cal Newport calls the thing you are building "career capital" — a stock of rare and valuable skills that you can spend on the things that make work worth doing: autonomy, impact, the ability to choose your problems. You do not negotiate for those with enthusiasm. You buy them with competence.

That framing put words to something I had already been doing without naming it. Every move I have made was a deposit, then a withdrawal. Build skills, cash them in for a harder game, build again.

Why I keep changing lanes

On paper my path looks restless. Civil engineer. International marketing across fifteen countries. Real estate, where my team did RM120 million in group sales in 2021. Now data and AI, building systems at Gruda and coaching senior leaders at LEAD. People ask what happened.

What happened is that none of those were passion jumps. Each one was a place to compound a skill I wanted and then trade it up. Engineering taught me to hold a whole system in my head and design for the load, not the demo. Sales taught me that systems only work if a human actually wants to use them. Data and AI let me put both together at a scale that was not possible before. The materials changed — concrete, conversations, code — but I was never chasing a feeling. I was chasing the next harder thing I could get good at.

If I had waited to feel passionate before moving, I would still be waiting. Instead I moved when I had enough capital to be useful in the new lane, and the passion caught up every time.

The bench

The craftsman mindset is unglamorous in practice. It is showing up to the bench and getting measurably better at something specific. Writing the SQL until the query is not just correct but clean. Designing the pipeline so the hand-offs hold when the upstream changes its schema without telling you. Sitting with a problem long enough to understand it instead of reaching for the first clever answer.

The question I bring to every project is the same one I learned as a junior engineer staring at a structural drawing: what is this thing supposed to carry? Answer that honestly and the rest follows — the design follows from the load, the skill follows from the reps, and eventually the passion follows from the skill.

I am not against loving your work. I love mine. I just think you build your way into it. You do not find it lying around.
